## CHAPTER ONE: SILAS

### 1: VANLIFE

Starting out on a southernmost section of the Pacific Crest Trail, between Lake Arrowhead and Big Bear, I didn't see a single other hiker the entire way…fak, this is already boring. 

San Diego wasn’t boring. That’s where this begins, Imperial Beach, the southern border. I’d enrolled in college with the dream of becoming a journalist. I gave that dream my all at a very liberal arts college where I learned more about how corruption of the corporate owned media and the decline of the artform, than I did about going to work for a news outlet. 

But the loss of that dream gave rise to a new one. I was gonna write the great American novel about vanlife. Imperial Beach to Alaska. Boarder to boarder on four wheels. The entire west coast would be my eggshell, clamshell, oyster, whatever. I had a framework, a working title. 

The day I left school, I found a book on the art of storytelling in a free pile outside the library. It was sat on top of a soup strainer half covered by a sweatshirt bearing the college logo. CRAFT: A Collection of Lessons, by Patricia Wildman. It was a small book, bound with twine and too much glue. CRAFT was full of lessons on writing and quotes from renowned authors. On the back, it said how Patricia put the entire book together during a single quarter ILC.

A to CRAFT, the first rule of effective storytelling is to not spend a lot of time on backstory, right at the outset. CRAFT says, a lot of first time authors make this mistake. Says not to do an entire chapter on where your characters grew up and all that. Says you risk killing the momentum of the story and that you’ve gotta just, “drop the reader straight into the action.” 

***

I was parked a block from the sand in Imperial Beach, feeding the meters and abusing the two-hour signs, watching for three-hour spots, which were nice, but by nature harder to come by. On the southern edge of the California coast, in San Diego County, Imperial Beach is the end of the line. Home to over three million people, any part of San Diego at midday is a human zoo, jam packed with locals and tourists alike. 

I suppose I’m somewhere in between. Silas Martin. 5’10, medium build, light hair, ? eyes. Jeans, tshirts, tennis shoes, typical white guy. I’ve been told I have white privilege (mostly by other white kids at Evergreen…never before that). To the extent that I understand what white privilege is, I stand in solidarity with the less fortunate and I suppose, also can’t deny it. 

Solidarity is a word that got used a lot at The Evergreen State College. And to the extent that I understand the way it got used, I’m all for it. Me, I’ve never really been part of a group of people to be in solidarity with. I’m an average kid from a no-name town in northern California that my parents moved us to, after moving to Maryland for a year after leaving Pennsylvania. You get the idea. Fair to say I became independent at a early age. 

Here in imperial Beach, making notes in the van and paying for parking instead of rent, I’m immersed this Southern California beach community and wondering if I feel like a part of it? I’d say I do. I mean, I’m out here all day, on the streets, in the mix. Sitting now, with my bare heels against the floor of the van, leaning back into the couch with my notebook open on the folding desk, I pulled back the curtain to peek out.

I’ve been on this block all morning. Slept around the corner from here last night. Chose this area ‘cause it’s mostly condos and apartments and probably half AIRB&Bs. No one knows who all lives here or might be visiting. Everyone besides me comes and goes. As the hours pass, I’m getting to know every crack in the sidewalk and the bark of every tree. I start to take on a certain sense of…not ownership but perhaps equity. Equity is a word that was used a lot at…no, stop. 

According to the Captain’s log: It’s a breezy, late February day. Windows cracked. The next pack of tourists pass, followed by the gentle bump of a meter maid chalking my tire. This means I officially have two full hours in the spot. I set my watch. The three-wheeled golf cart buzzes off and I unstrap my surfboard from the ceiling, add some wax and shoot out to catch a wave. 

Caught one, mostly got smashed on. I’m still learning. 

Back at the van, I strapped the board up and moved to a new spot. New signs, new block, I waited for the chalk, set the timer and hit the nearest coffee shop to get some writing done. Cafe was a bit fancy for my taste. Black aprons, white tile countertops and overpriced ristretto shots. Got a bit self-conscious like, I don’t really have money. I don’t even have a job. Like, they know I shouldn’t be here. Like, I’m not really working on my laptop. I’m not a writer. Standard issue imposter syndrome, I guess.

Maybe it was all in my head. You gotta learn to acknowledge when a thing is all in your head. But another point worth making is: clean clothes and careful grooming go a long way when you’re in a van. You basically want to do whatever you can to give off the impression you’re not. 

Left the cafe, moved the van again, fed the meter and grabbed a bite. After eating, I only had to repark once more before the signs timed-out and the ticket writers went home for the night. After that I grabbed a beer to celebrate my freedom to relax and watch the sunset for as long as I liked.

Number five on a long list of concerns around pursuing a career in journalism is: having no free time. Working eighty hour weeks, working weekends, working nights, pressure and deadlines.

As for vanlife, well, number two on the list of concerns (after not getting towed) is access to restrooms. It’s an aspect of the artform you might want to carefully map. Libraries, parks, public works offices, bus stations and travel shops. There are plenty of options if one seeks them out and…doesn’t abuse the spot. 

Parking by the beach is higher stakes you might say. It comes at an extra cost. But it offers two major advantages. One, it’s a busy public area that’s nearly impossible to track. Two, there are always public restrooms. Regardless, the number one rule of vanlife is: don’t abuse the spot. 

I’d been in San Diego for a week, learning to surf, getting pumped about making it to Alaska and trying to get a fire lit beneath this writing project. During that time, I never parked on the same block two nights in a row (if ever two nights at all). You learn to calculate your visibility, like who might see you and how often. Your target audience is essentially no one. What I would do is, wherever I planned to sleep, I wouldn’t pull into that spot until late. I’d pull in after most people were not even awake. Then in the morning, instead of stumbling out to stretch, I’d get up, start the engine and move to a fresh spot (probably near one of those bathrooms I’d mapped). 

Night before last, I found a slice good grey curb no one cared about beside an empty lot. Great. But spots like that are a double-edged sword. They can also expose you, like for instance, to the cops. You stick out, all alone like that. Anyone who sees you there overnight will take note. Me, I feel safest blending in, in plain sight. I like a dark neighborhood with a tall fence or a long hedgerow. I’ll look for a middle ground between two properties, such that it’s not clear which one I may or may not be at. That kind of grey area can give a van goer enough of a buffer to have their time ane vanish with one more safe night’s sleep under their hood (legal or not). Alright I think it’s time to get on with this hike. 


### 2: Backstory

Setting out on a southernmost section of the illustrious PCT, between Lake Arrowhead and Big Bear, I didn't see a single other backpacker the entire time…what, is the hike already over? Damnit, how am I supposed to start this thing? 

In journalism, you start with what’s known as ‘the lead’. The lead is the most important line in the story. The structure of a news article is kinda like a pyramid. The lead comes first, with the rest of the details opening outward from there in diminishing importance. This way, the reader can catch the essence straight off and then feel free to stop reading whenever, if they get bored.

Novels aren’t like that though. They’re supposed to hook you in right away. CRAFT says there’s a trick for doing this, one you see a lot in film and television. How it works is, you put a clip from the most nail-biting scene, right up front, before anything else. So like, let’s say: The van breaks down and Silas’s only hope is to make it to some sketchy automotive shop in rural Washington State. He’s almost there when he gets stopped at gunpoint by some backwoods nazi punks. You see him, me, behind the wheel, nervous and sweating and reaching beneath the seat for you don’t know what, does he have a gun? then cut—back to the PCT. The hook I guess, ensures that for the next two hundred pages, even if the story sucks, you’ll keep reading, to find out what happens with the terrorist attack. Yeah, no, not doing that.

Okay this is it: Strapping on my pack to conquer a southernmost part of the Pacific Crest Trail, which spans the entire west coast from California to Canada, I never did see another backpacker anywhere, just a lot of sun, rocks and sand. Bah, no, that’s still not it. Why is my life like one perpetual pattern of starting over?

Both my parents died young (young for them) both of cancer. They were both good people and both criminals in their own ways (in more ways than I’ll ever know). They did plenty wrong, to each other let’s say, cause I witnessed that much first hand, but also to their own bodies, smoking and drinking and eating fast and frozen food dinners every night and feeding them to me and my sister, Serene. 

“What are we having tonight mom?” She’d light a smoke and hand me ten bucks. “Here, you can walk to Jack in the Box.” Again? She’d take a drag, sip her drink and get back to her show. Wonderful woman though, don’t get me wrong. Heart of gold. Dad too. As far as losing them, I don’t know. I read somewhere it can make a man more independent. That’s a nice word fo it. But more independent than I already was? It was already just me and my sister and she was always off on her own writing her poetry. 

Mom died first. After dad, Serene went back to Pennsylvania, to be with the rest of our family. But not me, I stayed. 

I like it out west. To me, the wide expanses and open roads speak of adventure. The east coast is older, more filled in and more set in its ways. This side of the country feels full of possibly. Like, you can go anywhere you want. Like you can make something out of nothing if you give it your all. There's a sense that no one quite knows what’s gonna happen next. I like that. So I stayed. I stayed in Northern California with my few friends from highschool. 

The first chance I had to get out of there, I took a road trip with my buddy Pete. We drove down to Big Sur and had a blast. On the drive back, Pete talked my ear off about how he was gonna propose to his girlfriend and the apartment they were gonna get. He told me all about his job and upcoming promotion. I was single. None of my relationships had ever lasted more than two months. I had no plan, no job skills, no calling in life, at least none that I was aware of. 

I thought I’d found a calling in journalism. And maybe I did. But one thing I learned (not from school) is that to get a job writing for a media outlet, what you need are existing credentials. They don’t gives two cents about your hard earned degree. Editors and newspaper execs only want to know where you’ve worked already and what you’ve written to date. 

That trip to Big Sur was magical, but when we got back to our little town, it was so lackluster, so boring. Plus I was broke. So I started stealing. Small stuff at first. Stuff I knew I could get away with. It was just so easy. Plus it added some excitement to things. I only ever stole enough to get by. Only one car. And I didn’t keep it long. I always worked alone and never got caught. It was the one time I brought a friend that I did. Travis. That shitty little corner store and all for like three hundred bucks. 

He and I watched from outside until the kid working went in back. Would’ve gone off fine if it wasn’t for Trav. He was just supposed to watch the door, that’s it. I hit the till in a flash. But he had to start grabbing stuff off the shelves and dropped a box of whip cream cartridges and that was that. Kid heard us and called the cops. We ran but didn’t get far. 

So that’s what I went down for: for bringing a friend. Whatever kind of sideways justice that is, or was, it’s over now. I’m out. Time served. Spose my spree of petty theft had to end somehow. 

I only did like three months. The jail was full, I guess. These days I wouldn’t steal a pack of gum. If you love your country, why would you steal from it? Why did I…well, I was young and dumb and desperate, I guess. Number one reason to not commit another crime as long as you live: don’t buy into a false sense of desperation. You hold the keys to your own self worth, even if you’re broke or homeless or live in a van.

Anyway, in jail, you hear stories. Some funny ones. But mostly sad ones and dark ones. For most people, those would be the number one reason to keep their hands clean. Me, I just wanted to write them all down. Talking to people in jail turned a light on in my brain. Like, this is the real world. Not: pay your taxes, put up a white picket fence and everything’s gonna be fine. In jail, there was a different level of honesty about human vice and virtue, one that rarely comes out until the criminal in us gets caught. And rarely even then. Anyway, I guess I saw the value in it. 

As soon as I was out, I made a commitment: I was going to write. I figured the material I needed was all around me. I figured jail isn’t even half the criminals on earth, it’s just all the poor people who got caught.

Unless someone comes to pick you up, the jail puts you on a bus and lets you off in a random city, halfway across the state. They let me off in Fresno. I’d just turned 23. I wanted nothing to do with Fresno, or my former town, so I hitchhiked up to Washington State. I’d heard good things about Olympia. I decided that was where to begin my new life. From then on, I was all about notebooks and coffeeshops. And I found a cash gig, mowing lawns. 

Mowing lawns was worse work than I ever would’ve guessed. It’s one thing to mow your own. But putting in eight hour days with two-stroke gas engines vibrating up your arms and pouring toxic fumes into your lungs where the only break to get is is to get into the stinky diesel truck that hauls the machines and clipping and chug off to the next job…I’d be a spaced out ghost by the end. And that grass just keeps coming. “All summer, there’s as much work as you can stomach,” the crew boss would say.

Honestly, it’s the kind of work you just want to forget. I’m almost sorry I mentioned it. But I cut enough grass to buy a used van. Finally the main attraction. It was a bit of a beater but it beat sleeping in a tent. It was some tile contractor’s old work van. Had a lot of miles but I liked it. The van was painted a faded light-brown, like he color of the desert. There were these little sun bursts where the paint had oxidized in spots. The logo for the tile company had read: Walker Imperial Tile. They got most of the lettering scrubbed off, but you could still see the faded W and the I and the T misaligned across the one side.

I paid cash and waited for a nice hot day. I took it to a car wash and took a high powered sprayer up and down and inside and out. I hit the floor, the roof, the seats, everything. An hour later it was clean and dry. Road worthy, we rolled up on my favorite coffee spot. Arora, my favorite barista, she takes one look at the van and goes, “Thing looks like it has plenty of power to haul mowers and clippings.” Arora, my first sorta friend in Olympia, she says, “You could start your own business Si.” Mowers? Clippings? I just fuggn cleaned this thing. 

Arora was sweet and had kind eyes. She had flowers and butterflies tatted across her chest. Her hair was always dyed. I thought of her as fairy-like. The first time she came out from behind the counter, I noticed her body. Mid-sized, pretty nice. But that’s not the main attraction here, at least not yet. “I’m just saying,” she said, “you could make more money if you went into business for yourself.” Like I said, I hated doing lawns. And I had no way to make money at it, but I knew I wanted to write. 

Arora only worked part-time. She was also a student and one night after she got off work, she invited me to take a walk across the campus. 

Summer nights in Washington can stay light out ‘till ten at night. That evening, we walked the campus paths between the concrete buildings and rolling lawns. She told me how her school was liberal and political and free. “You can study anything at Evergreen,” she said. Aurora goes, “You can make up your own ILC.” “What’s an ILC?” I asked. “An Independent Learning Contract. Basically it’s a full quarter academic program that you design yourself.” 

“You should go here,” she lit up. She added, “anyone can get in.” The next day I went back on my own and talked to an academic dean who told me to talk to financial aide. They told me I’d be eligible for student loans and that it would be enough money to pay for tuition and rent and books and everything. It suddenly seemed like I could go to Evergreen. Me an education. Me, a degree. I told my sister back east and she helped me fill out all the paperwork over email. 

It all happened fast. The money came through. Fall quarter was around the corner and what do you know, I got in. Arora was pumped. Serene was stoked. I was going to college for journalism. At least that was my major, my focus, as the school called it. I wanted this and bad. I was on fire the first week of class. I was gonna get a job on the front lines of life, covering world events, covering natural disasters, covering crime. I’d be covering I didn’t know what, but in four years I’d have a degree and a career, or could have, if I hadn’t dropped out.

Silas looked away from his notebook and down at his hiking boots. Below a fresh layer of dust and a weed caught in his laces, they were brand new. Big mistake. He looked back down at the ink still drying on his spiral bound page and coached himself to stop. “Aren’t you here to write about the hike and the trail ?” He clicked his pen back on or out.

Hiking a part of the Pacific Crest Trail, between Lake Arrowhead and Big Bear, I never did see another backpacker, just a lot of dirtbikes, jeeps and lifted SUVs. Man, I honestly don’t know if I’m even on the PCT right now. Pretty sure I’m close. 

It would’ve been cool to quit school and make straight for the trail. What a bold new beginning. Like, fuck the establishment, I’m going hiking. Doing that might’ve crossed my mind. Problem was, I had money. Left Evergreen with some left over student loan funds. A fair amount of them. The walk away money I started calling it. Real low on the list of reasons to drop out of school for journalism (like number ninety nine) is to get in your van and fly south with the walk away funds.

So there I was in Imperial Beach, getting away with vanlife somewhere I could never afford and getting lined out for my two thousand mile journey north. That first week man, I was crushing it, or so I believed. I wasn’t crushing it at surfing, because it’s really hard, but I’d stood up on a few waves and hadn’t drown. Then I broke the board. The honeymoon phase with the writing petered out and I began drinking more, a lot more, let’s say. Like, I got the ball rolling and started drinking just to fill the time. I bottomed out. And I did do something about it. But first, I need to tell you about my last night in Imperial Beach. That night, I met someone. 

I was alone on a picnic bench in the beer garden of the Stone Brewing Company. I sat there, doubting the novel and second-guessing my ability to even make it to Alaska. I was feeling like a bum, like a privileged punk out to glorify his homelessness, on a state funded escapade to, well, right now the bar. I sat drinking and thinking about how there was no one even in this story besides me. Boring dude. Plus who was I, this average white guy with average intelligence to be writing a novel about himself? However, people did have an above average tendency to open up and talk to me. It happened a lot in jail. It’s one of the reasons becoming a journalist made sense to me. 

Sitting and sulking and sipping my Stone IPA, I noticed this young woman moving between tables like she knew everyone in the place. She was hard to miss, with her hair in patches of peach and bright green between choppy lengths of brown. I kept hearing her voice. Coarse, confident, loud. I froze as she stepped up to my bench and that fast, became the evening’s main attraction. 


### Part 3: ADDIE 

Addie stepped up in boots and black jeans. Her jeans were tight. Her tshirt bore the image of a fist raised in protest and had a few small rips. She stood looking down at me and my notebook like this was her bench, her patio, like she owned the place, or more accurately, like no one did. 

“Saw you last night,” she said, using her tongue like a whip. “I was here,” I told her, rolling up one sleeve but lowering my head a notch, embarrassed about my heavy drinking. Apparently my response was fine ‘cause she sat right down with her drink, put her hand out and said, “Addie.”  

“Silas,” I said, taking it.

Her grip was firm. I noticed her nose, lips and ears were all small and unusually square. Her eyes were keen. Her cheeks looked soft. She shook my hand and spoke and moved like a soft machine. 

She? “I don’t much care for female pronouns,” Addie said. “But you know what Silas, I don’t think I care that much about pronouns at all.” A refreshing take after what an ordeal they’d been at Evergreen. 

“We’re all a bit self-obsessed these days,” she addded. 
I raised a brow. 
“If you ask me, the western world has taken its individualism a bit too far.”
“How do you mean?” I asked her.
“Impulse consumerism. Identity politics.” She looked down her nose at me.

I wouldn’t call Addie butch, but for a girl, definitely stong. She sat upright, looking straight at me. When she spoke, her voice was like wood, with every grain of doubt or hesitation sanded out. But I sipped my beer and started to relax. I could read her body language and see her interest. 

Addie glanced down at my notebook. She asked what I write.
“I’m just getting started,” I said hesitatantly, adding, “I write about life, I guess.” 
“Life,” she echoed. “But what do you stand for Silas?” 
“Not fiction,” I blurted out, “and not poetry.” What was I saying? “I want to write about about the real world,” I told her.  
Addie raised her mug and said, “Cheers to that.” Her mouth might’ve creased into a smile but I can’t say for sure. She sipped her drink, squared her lips and said, “I’m an anarchist.”

Some half-conscious inhibition dropped out of me in the instant of her saying that. She took another sip. Her lips were wet and for a moment I imagined kissing them. “I went to school for journalism,” I told her, “but I dropped out.” I had to stop myself. I’d said it to impress her, which felt gross. “Now I’m working on a novel,” dude stop.

I took my last sip and offered to get us another round. Back with drinks I asked, “Don’t we at least need some amount of government to keep crime down?” I added that this was coming from someone whose been to jail.”

“What’d you do?”
“Petty theft, a few months.”
“You seem like a good guy Silas. I’d bet you didn’t steal because you had no morals and needed to be punished.”
“I mean, I was broke. I didn’t even have an address and didn't see how to get a job without one.”

“Kind of,” I admitted. I liked her voice. I liked listening to her talk.

“People think we need government to keep crime down. And to some extent they do. But power corrupts, it’s just a matter of time. Consider the very short political history of the U.S.” 
“Go on,” I scribbled. 
“Wealthiest country in the world and with every passing year, we get more poverty, more debt, more citizens in jail, more rich politicians and more people fighting for their lives just to get by.”  

I stopped my pen. “What is Anarchy then? Isn’t is basically chaos? How is that a solution?”
“Freedom, Addie said, “that’s all it is.” She stood. “People call it chaos and call it disorder, yet countless societies have worked out their own economic systems without government control.” 
“Really, like who?”
“Like eight million people in Catalonia. Like Chiapas, Mexico. Like the Native tribes of this land and,” she paused, “so many others. More than we’ll ever know. The only reason they don’t last is  some armed state or another comes marching in to overthrow them and take control.”

Addie sat back down and took a sip. “The writers of history call every stateless society primitive, Silas.” I agreed that they mostly did. “But it’s human life we’re talking about,” she said, “wild, brilliant and flawed…it’s us,” she addeed. I felt her boot bump my shoe beneath the bench.

Before meeting Addie, before Imperial Beach and the PCT, I’d been in school for nearly two full academic years and I did give it all I had. If you’re wondering why he didn’t just start this hike at the border, at the beginning of thePCT, where he already was, well, he read about that section of the trail crossing open desert in the full sun. “Steep arid hills, deadly exposure and gruelling heat.”

Furthermore Si had no desire to further his luck in San Diego County. So he picked a different trailhead. He told himself this would be the beginning, of his book and his roadtrip north, into the land of the midnight sun. “The beginning of the rest of my life,” he wrote, and a long sober run. 

Northbound two hours then. He followed the map’s blue line. Si didn’t trust the phone to get him to the trail but he took some screenshots to go with. He read a yelp review that said he could get on the PCT not far from the Ranger Station in Arrowhead. If nothing else, he decided, the Rangers would advise him and sell him a proper map.

	Si reached the station where were no Rangers to consult and no maps to buy or even look at. There was a small wooden building. It was closed. 

He stood around with pack packed, fumbling with a lock of his light brown hair and trying to stop thinking about going into town for a trail beer. A local girl on her way to school saw him and stopped. Emily rolled down her window and called out. “You okay? Need some help?” 

Emily was a senior in high school but she knew Lake Arrowhead well. She knew the famous trail ran a mile east of her own backyard and told Si, “I don’t know exactly where it is. But I do know this trailhead where people park and there’s a map posted there.” That sounds great, he said.

Si followed Emily to the end of a county maintained road. They stood together at the bottom of a canyon and examined the map. “This is Deep Creek,” she said, pointing to the X marking the spot. “How far do you plan to go,” she asked. “I plan on hiking for two days to get to Big Bear. Then I’ll resupply and go on.” Emily was sweet. She cautioned and encouraged him. She handed Si a scrap of paper with her number and said, “I’ll be thinking about you. Stay safe and text me when you’re out.” He pulled the van beneath the branches of a giant pepper tree and made his start. 

Emily’s car vanished up the mountain road in a cloud of dust. Si stepped up to the laminated sign. Wide-eyed, he studied all the trails and paths and roads in the immediate area. There were blue lines and white lines and dotted lines. The PCT was there. It wasn’t close, but was on the map. And it was the OHV road to Crab Flats looked like his best and most direct shot. 


***

The road to Crab Flats was two and a half hours of pure elevation gain. No shade and nothing flat. With a full load of food and water, gear and first aid, the young man set out.

His pack at its heaviest, he went sidestepping up the sloping wall of sand and loose rock. Every step was steep and uneven. Most of the road was in the shape of a W, heavily worn by rubber and rain. For walking up with a full size backpack, this was not ideal terrain. But it was the start of his hike and he’d never been on the PCT and he was pumped and just wanted to get there. So he charged full boar up this mangled mountain thing.  

The road remained steep and stayed mangled. Up and around and then down for a quick minute and then up around the side of a giant boulder and then up up up lots more. 

He hadn't taken note elevation gains or exact distances on the map. He kept thinking he might be almost to the top, only to crest and find the sandy, busted road climbing into the foreseeable future for as far as he could tell. Pushing and pacing, over the next ridge with the sun beating down, grunting and complaining out loud he reminded himself, This was just the beginning. “Soldier fourth and don’t look up.” Eyes on the sand, sweat dripping, at times, on all fours. And by the time he reached Crab Flats his legs were two boiled noodles, stuck to themselves.

He took off his pack beneath a tree and flopped his back against the trunk. The Flats spread out before him for a quarter-mile in three directions. Even from a sitting position, he was so high up, he could see the tops of the surrounding ranges, down into San Bernardino basin and clear out across the Inland Empire.

The Flats doubled as a parking lot for the adventurous folks who drove up here and there were a few cars. There was a campground. There were some paths leading off. There was another map posted on one corner of the plateau which he was certain would inform him as to the very nearby location of the PCT. For now, it was just good to be off his feet. He got out his notebook and spent however long, under his tree, resting and reading through his notes on Mexico.


***

“A long sober run,” he wrote. Silas gave a final nod to his season of drinking and to something inherently empty, that had driven it. He raised his head to Rico and friends and the way they’d made him care about being at his best, and also about them.

He looked over at the trail sign. “Ready to go.” Si stepped out of the shade and into the midday sun. Hot. “A cold beer right now would, ”No.” He took a swig from one of his jugs. Refreshing. He approached the map and read over multiple times. All the roads and trails were all labeled in forest service codes like: B10W6, which was fine, except that none the numbers and letters on the map were matching the numbers and letters on the actual roads and trails around Crab Flats. After failing to make sense of it, walking back and fourth down this track and that, without his pack, just searching for any sign of the PCT, Silas headed into the campground to see if someone there could help. 

In what seemed a stroke of luck he found two San Bernardino County Forest Rangers checking permits in the campground. They looked like they could’ve been brothers, with their badges and ticket books and an aire of being on a boring vacation. He greeted them and asked them and they didn't know where the Pacific Crest Trail was but they checked their maps and found it.  

The Rangers showed Si a solid yellow line labeled Y11P4. The older brother pointed at a patch of trees on the north corner of the Plateau. “It's a single track," he said. So not another OHV road, thank god. “That’d be your best bet for gettin on the trail,” the younger sibling said. 

Well, Y11P4 was a dirt bike road which (spoiler) is what finished off Si’s legs. It dropped straight back down the mountain and was even steeper, sketchier and more unhikeable than the road up. With the weight of his pack, he went skidding down that single track, jumping small rocks and fighting to stay on two feet. Slipping on loose sand, grabbing branches and roots to slow his momentum, just trying so hard not to roll an ankle, but what other option did he have? He hiked or slid or dirt surfed down down down. And when he finally did reach the PCT, at the bottom of Holcomb Valley, he could hardly walk. 

But you live and learn. One thing you learn is that the Pacific Crest Trail was designed to be hiked from south to north, not vice versa. So now SIlas was on the trail and it was following a creek and there were oaks and willows and grass and shade and a cool breeze. But after about a mile, the trail ran out onto another OHV road. Standing in the middle of it, he found a post at his back, carved with the three lovely letters, but no indication of where to get back on to continue south.  

Si searched and searched and gave up. The trail was obviously around there somewhere. But he couldn’t find it. A dirt bike screamed by. He held his breath for the cloud of dust it left. A Land Rover hauling a family of four came rollicking in from the other side. The cloud that left filled the roadway and bled into the surrounding forest.

Sweaty and sore, rechecking this path and that in search of any indication of where the trail might pick up, he eventually approached a young lumbering off roader, out with his wife and daughter and a twelve pack in their lifted Suburban. “Hey there,” Si said, “Might you happen to know where the Pacific Crest Trail picks up, heading south?” 

"Pacific Crest Trail,” his voice booming from a barrel chest. “Whut's that? Never heard of it."  
“Well it’s a hiking trail,” Si told him. “It runs from Canada to Mexico and we're basically on it.”  
"So you been walking since Canada?"  
“Naw man, just Lake Arrowhead.”  
Si said how they made a movie about it, about this girl who hiked most of it, “called Wild.”
"Yeah" the man said, looking left, "I saw that’n the theater. Musta been pretty drunk.”  
“Oh.” 
“Anywho bud, sorry I can't help ya none. But good luck."  
Si walked off and heard him holler back to base, "Hell honey, he's hiking all the way to Canada."    


The sun was beating down. Silas had two ballcaps on, spread out across his brow to form a doublewide brim. He wore a button up shirt with the collar flipped up to protect the back of his neck. Inside the boots he’d failed to break in, fresh blisters throbbed with the beat of his heart.

He found a rock and sat making notes about the hike so far. He recalled a lesson from CRAFT: “Your description should appeal to all five senses,” it said. It said to, “Imagine the body as a more effective recording devise than the mind.” Huh. He ate a half an apple and some cheese. The cheese was, soft. The apple was fresh. The airborne dust getting in his mouth along with them was dry and sour. He sat scratching his head to the soft sound of dirt bikes in the distance. A sharp part of the rock was poking into his butt and causing part of it to go numb.

An afternoon jeepgoer rolled through. He had a big bushy mark twain mustache. He saw Si’s blue backpack leaned up on the rock and slowed to offer a bottle of water from his ice chest. Brief Heaven. Si queried the man and with a sparkle in his eyes, through his wall of whiskers, he said, “I do believe you can get on that trail about three quarters of a mile up this road.” He pointed up the OHV road. “Okay,” Si sighed and thanked him for the water and eventually, did that.  

Trekking on, the water was warm by the last few sips. Si’s jugs were strapped to his pack, which was a lot to carry, but water wasn’t something he was taking chances on. Hiking on, his pack grew heavier as his pace grew slower. His right knee hurt. His left leg felt like it had a molten cable running from that ankle, up to where the weight of the pack rode on his hip. Pushing forth, he started squeaking out strange noises to cope with the tension and aches and burning.

Si didn't mind the pain. But a bitterness was setting in about his not being on the trail still. “The PCT had a vibe.” This OHV felt dead to him. Three quarters of a mile came and went. The road mr mustache had him going up was running in S curves, which meant half his steps were in the opposite direction the other half. “Should at least getting closer to Big Bear,” he hoped. 


***

Around the next rocky bend, the road fed Silas and his pack out into a wide swath of open land. This entire forest valley had severely burned the year before, so again, no shade at all. 


Through that forest of burnt sticks, squinting beneath his double brim hat system and singing folk songs to the lizards, imagining his legs might be made of metal or air—anything to encourage them, he unclipped his jugs and carried one in each hand—anything to get some weight off his back. Up, over and through as the afternoon sun hit a rocky ridge, he found the PCT again, marked with a small brown sign. It was running through a swath of tall grass between a stand of healthy, unburnt dwarf pines. 
 
Smiling again, he trekked on, past silver boulders and flowering sagebrush. There was a pond lined with cattails. There were live oaks with bluebirds darting between. The landscape had come to life again and the track friendly to tread upon. 

“This is what I came for,” Si told himself, feeling a sweet combination of direction  and freedom. The thought of a cold beer had left his mind and he took a nice pull from one of his warm jugs. 

When the trail began to climb, it saddled a high ridgeline. This was suddenly everything he’d hoped it would be. The path of the PCT was determined and intuitive. It held a lineage of adventure, he believed and Si stopped to make note. “As water carves canyons, the footsteps of every hiker before me, carved this wanderlust I feel.” You’re one click away from poetry there bud. “I’m honored,” he penned on, “to be one more drop in the course of it’s living history.”

At this point in his story, Silas was hiking for about five minutes at a time, then stopping to rest for nearly as long. His legs were wrecked. It was starting to get dark. He was desperate to peel off his pack and call it a day. But the ridge he was on hadn't had a patch of flat ground in ages. 

When the narrow trail began to wind into a steep canyon, Si started to worry he could misstep and go stumbling down to his death. He checked one of his screenshots. There was a little tent on it and he and indulged a fantasy about there being a campground up ahead. Sun down. Hats off. Stars out. Headlamp on, as the milky way stretched out across the valley overhead. Some sort of small animal moved in the brush. An owl watched him pass. Above the next bend, the glowing eyes of a young coyote, stared down off its rocky perch. An act surrender then. 

The moon rose out of the dark woods. Si pulled his pen. “If we persevere long enough,” he wrote, “across whatever hardship we face, is there a point when the struggle wears off? The complaints stop coming. The mind quits bothering us about how much further it will be. A dialogue with the body, about comfort and stress, comparing this sensation to that, brakes down and is replaced by a willingness to walk on.” 

Walking however, was no longer an accurate description of what the man was doing. Even limping would be strong. He was more like lifting one wasted leg at a time, from its spot in the dirt, dragging it forward and setting it down and with a dramatic thud before lifting the next. 

Every seven seconds, a few feet further into eternity. If he’s gonna write poetry, I can. The stars looked down. The canyon smiled up. Over the hills and through the night, our man trekked on until finally, eventually reaching the…“Hey, wait, what the hell is this?” Silas heard a voice. He caught a flash of electric light. A car door closed and the car drove off. He saw the sign, the road, the pepper tree, the van. “I’m back where I started? That mark twain mustache mother...”

And those were young Silas’s last words of the night. Under the white light of the waning moon, he limped up to the door fo van, dug out his keys and climbed in. Si slipped his sleeping bag up over his tired bones hit the hay. He wan’t sure he’d ever needed a good night’s sleep this bad. His awareness quickly drifted and he dreamt about powerful salmon and strange insects. 

***

In the morning, he thought about repacking and driving to a different trailhead to try again. But who was he kidding, he was in no shape for that. He texted Emily, started Wit, and drove into Arrowhead.
